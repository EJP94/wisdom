> **In short** — When something starts hurting, it's almost never that thing that's the root cause, but a tight or weak connected or opposite muscle: low back pain so often comes from tight glutes and hamstrings, or from sitting too much, which tightens the psoas. Think push versus pull, front versus back, left versus right, and try rolling out to find tight spots.

*Ideas from Holistic Alpha, as told by host Steven Mathis and, where marked, his guests and listeners. Numbers in parentheses are episodes. Sources he credits are named where they appear. Not fact-checked.*

## Pain comes from somewhere else
- Over the years, one finding has been kind of counterintuitive but almost universally true: when something starts hurting, it's almost never that thing that's the actual root cause. **Story:** thankfully no serious injuries, just little things here and there, some taking a few weeks to recover from (007)
- When your low back hurts, so often it's not your back at all that's weak or tight, but usually your glutes and hamstrings: tight, they pull on your low back (007)
- So often you feel the pain but aren't conscious of the tightness: unless you're closely paying attention, it's easy to miss that the area next to it, or balancing it, is kind of the root cause (007)
- Things that can make your low back hurt: standing or being on your feet a really long time, and things that really engage your glutes and hamstrings, especially if you're not used to it (007)
- Often, sitting too much tightens the front of the hips, the psoas muscles, throwing your body kind of out of balance front to back and causing back pain often enough. When the psoas, a really deep abdominal muscle, is tight, people probably extremely rarely feel pain there: what hurts is what it throws out of balance (007)

## Balance in the body
- Balance in life, and in the body: see [[_parked-mind#Balance in life]] (007)
- **Story:** climbing almost daily, a lot at the gym, worked the upper-body pull muscles a lot while the push muscles didn't carry their weight, which overstressed the pull muscles and made them sore. Adding push-ups consistently, now alternated with climbing on a lot of solo days, made the climbing better, harder, longer and less painful, with a stronger feeling, because of the balance. Not surprising: too much pulling makes push muscles weak comparatively (007)
- Balance between life's parts seems a little more obvious, maybe because we hear and think about it more, though a reminder still helps. Balance within the body, which affects us a lot, we really don't seem to hear a lot about (007)
- Think about connected things and the opposite side, balance-wise: push versus pull, front versus back, left versus right. So often the root cause is not the one you think (007)

## What to do
- The encouragement: when something is tweaked, sore or hurting, consider that it may be a balance thing, the opposite or connected muscle, maybe the next muscles in the chain. With your back, it's not a front-to-back thing: a lot of times it's above or below in the chain, usually below, like your glutes and hamstrings, and even your calves and feet, causing low back pain through tightness in that chain (007)
- You can try this: different rollers work well, but some kind of massage roller works great (personally, a lot of different ones get used). Rolling out your body reveals tight spots you weren't conscious of, and it's like, "whoa. That's really tight" (007)
- **Story:** realizing this helped with pain and soreness: overall health and wellness got tremendously better, feeling strong without tight, sore, painful areas, as it helped figure out what was going on and deal with it more long term (007)

## Recovery makes training sustainable
- Releasing pressure points and tightness in your muscles is so crucial for feeling good, performing and, more importantly, avoiding injury. Injury sucks: training that leads to injury leaves you worse off than not training (015)
- One of the most important personal keys to fitness: training in a sustainable, long-term way, whose maybe most important part is listening to your body: getting rid of tightness and pressure points, taking an active role in recovery instead of waiting while things build up (015)
- Staying on top of tightness and sore spots, you feel better and stronger and have longer, harder workouts. **Story:** tight muscles after a workout tend to mean worse sleep, maybe for you too. Tightness, a lot of times the kind we can't even really notice until we roll out, builds up until all of a sudden something gets really sore or injured. **Story:** the aim is to roll out at least a little every day (015)

## Be proactive
- Be proactive, the most important thing found with rollers, stretching and any recovery: wait until something's sore and it's too late. As they say, if you wait until you're thirsty to drink, you're already dehydrated; a pain signal means the area has gone beyond tight to really inflamed and hurting. Rolling over your main areas lets you find tight spots and address them before they get painful during activity (015)

## Rolling out: tools and how
- Rolling out, the favorite term: the foam roll has its place, but a couple of other tools seem a lot better and more versatile (015)
- **Story:** probably 15 different foam rolls, massage rollers, sticks and recovery tools were tried over the years, plus plenty of professional massages, and some have their place. The Roll Recovery, spring loaded with lacrosse wheels on both sides, is really awesome, but about 140 bucks and really only has sort of applications to kind of the big parts of your body. The favorite is the most basic and the very cheapest: the Gaiam Restore Pressure Point Massager, from the yoga wellness company, seven bucks on Amazon, probably 15 when bought, "the best seven bucks you've ever spent", used until a couple wore out. Keeping only it, without even professional massage, would be alright. Highly recommended, though any roller you have is a start (015)
- Its three spiky balls make it kind of a combination of acupuncture and the release of rolling, and, a personal favorite, it really helps you notice what is tight (015)
- **Story:** quick passes over the entire body, neck down through arms and legs, then working a little more into a sore, really tight spot with a pressure point, or using one of a couple of other favorite rollers (015)
- Otherwise, or in addition, use a foam roll, another roller or a professional massage. Recovery is so critical for keeping your body in the game long term, even if you're not training hard. So often, as people age, areas start to hurt, which seemingly largely doesn't have to happen: it's largely built-up tension and tightness, worse over time unaddressed (015)
- **Story:** before climbing that day, a good 20 minutes of rolling out everywhere loosened things up for a much, much better workout; some versions take two minutes or less of quick passes. The spiky balls' acupuncture kind of action really increases blood flow wherever you use it: perfect as a pre-workout warm-up, and great after, for loosening up and digging into sore spots (015)

## A relaxed body, a relaxed mind
- A promise: you will feel the benefits in your body, and really in your mind too. **Story:** tight hamstrings, quads and legs especially lead to sort of an uneasiness, and uneasiness in the body translates to the mind. The more relaxed our body, the more relaxed our mind, and the other way too. We hopefully relax the mind with meditation, exercise and enough sleep; releasing tightness feeds the relaxation loop back from the body, for feeling better physically and mentally (015)

> **TL;DR** — A hormone checklist to limit stress hormones and support good ones, for all of us, even more for endurance athletes: vegetables, key nutrients, healthy fats and water; sleep, deep breathing, touch, less ejaculation; strength training, sprints, no overtraining; adaptogens, nettle root, fenugreek and DHEA.

*Ideas and stories from Holistic Alpha episode 29, as told by host Steven Mathis. Sources he credits are named where they appear. Not fact-checked.*

- If you're not into an endurance sport, keep workouts to 45 minutes or less and focus on intensity: beyond that time frame we start to have a detrimental impact on our hormones. For endurance athletes, what follows applies even more, because of the added stress on their hormones, but it applies to all of us
- A quick checklist in four major areas. The hope: one, two or three things pop out in your unique circumstances to focus on right now for an immediate impact
- Two main negative hormonal impacts:
  - Excess stress hormones like cortisol and adrenaline. They serve a purpose, but a lot of us run with too many throughout most of our lives
  - Not making enough good hormones: testosterone specifically, if we're not supporting its production, or a general sort of hormone production fatigue, or adrenal fatigue. Both kinds of production issue can affect us at different times, but in general, doing the things that support hormone production supports all of it
- The four areas: nutrition, lifestyle, training and supplementation
- **Nutrition:**
  - Get enough micronutrients, the things in a multivitamin, but from real food, which our body can use more effectively. The number one place is vegetables, lots of them
  - Two real favorites: cabbage and broccoli, really high in nutrients and inhibiting excess estrogen, highly suggested. A third with an added boost: beets, really good for nitric oxide, which helps blood flow, physical performance and sexual performance
  - Vitamin D, magnesium and zinc are absolutely worth supplementing, so important for overall physical health and for hormones
  - Avoid foods with a negative impact. Number one is sugar, which spikes cortisol and reduces testosterone. Along with it, highly processed carbs and toxic fats, the polyunsaturated fats (some labels don't break it down enough). Number one to avoid: the sort of nasty oils, vegetable, canola, corn, peanut, safflower, sunflower. Pretty much avoid oils in products except a few: coconut oil, a really good source of saturated fat; organic red palm oil, both saturated and monounsaturated; olive oil, with proven benefits you've probably heard of, really good for hormones too
  - Getting enough healthy fats, saturated and monounsaturated, from clean sources, is maybe the most important nutritional thing for hormone production: they're the building blocks our body makes hormones with. Besides those three oils: pasture-raised dairy, avocados, eggs
  - Get enough water. Without it, the body redistributes some of that hydration to protect more important areas and starts reducing things like making sperm and testosterone
  - Support dopamine production (an earlier episode covers dopamine). Avocados, eggs, fish and chicken contain tyrosine; personally, a lot of avocados, eggs and fish. If you're not eating those, you may want to consider supplementing a little tyrosine
  - Probiotics and prebiotics, including foods like sauerkraut, kefir and kombucha, boost your gut, and a lot of your brain is in your gut. Studies show probiotics lower cortisol levels
- **Lifestyle:**
  - Number one, without question: **sleep**, your superpower, when the body recovers in so many ways. Not enough spikes cortisol, and most good hormone production, including testosterone, happens at night: a double whammy. That's the thing with most hormone matters: stress hormones and good hormones like testosterone, dopamine and oxytocin tend to have kind of an inverse relationship in a lot of cases, so some of these negatives, like hurting our sleep, kind of hit twice
  - Breathe more deeply. We often breathe really shallow and quickly; really breathing deep for a couple of minutes has been shown to really significantly lower stress hormones, including cortisol. Do it as often as you can: it seems too simple, but it's actually really effective
  - Have lots of sex and interpersonal touch of all kinds, in healthy relationships, including hugs and even handshakes, because they boost oxytocin. Often known as the love hormone, it's much more: it signals our stem cells to regenerate muscle, and some very interesting studies, covered in a recent oxytocin episode, really prove its fundamental health impact
  - Live active and get outside: lots of proven cortisol and stress hormone reduction benefits from nature. Getting outside and being active in any adventurous way, a hike, a climb, a ride, helps support your hormones
  - Don't ejaculate as often. Conserving your semen helps in so many ways, your life force, your energy, your hormones. This means primarily: don't ejaculate alone, start edging and learn to masturbate without ejaculation; with a partner, learn to separate orgasm and ejaculation. Other episodes go into detail
- **Training:** a lot of us get into kind of routines. Endurance athletes on multiple-hour runs or rides will find it really helpful to add these, but they're important for all of us:
  - **Strength training:** we need to move heavy stuff. It absolutely boosts testosterone; without it, testosterone simply isn't going to be where it could and should be. It doesn't have to take long. The number one key: compound exercises involving the majority of your body, moving a decent amount of weight. Most things with a kettlebell, deadlifts, squats, dumbbell snatches, slam balls
  - **Sprints:** two or three days a week, even just two, absolutely boost testosterone in almost no time. Warm up a little, then 2–3 minutes, 4, maybe 5 max, for a number of really good sprints. Sprints as short as 6 seconds have been shown to raise testosterone, and up to 30 seconds raised it for even longer. Working up to, say, five 30-second sprints a couple days a week is a good place to start, as long as you're in decent mobility
  - Don't overtrain. It will for sure raise cortisol and adrenaline, and eventually leads to injury, as it usually does, so all that training was for nothing. An interesting byproduct: pain raises cortisol, so sore all the time, your body is literally in a higher cortisol state. Rollers, stretching and massages minimize tightness and pain, so you feel and perform better and your hormonal levels physically benefit
- **Supplementation:** a few really recommended, worthwhile to check out for nearly all men. They do affect people differently, but in general they're safe and effective:
  - **Adaptogens:** a class of usually herbs that are basically safe and non-toxic, affect multiple body systems, and help us deal with excess stress, physical, mental or both, their primary role. Five personally found amazing; they work slightly differently for different people and at different dosages, so a suggestion: give them a try and see how they work
    - Maca: a great place to start for any libido or sex drive boost, with positive effects on overall physical capacity and dealing with stress. Absolutely one of the favorites
    - Rhodiola: absolutely recommended to check out, especially for endurance athletes. It grows at high altitude and a number of high-altitude peoples, including Sherpas and others adapted to high altitude, use it because it helps the body in a lot of ways, including oxygen uptake. Amazing for mood and reducing stress, for all of us
    - Ashwagandha: maybe the favorite for lowering cortisol, very effective and proven for it. Running in a high cortisol state, especially with sleep issues from it, it will very likely help you sleep
    - Ginseng, grouped with eleuthero, maybe a step below the first three, both very worthwhile. Some people find them more energizing, some more calming
    - Adaptogens have different impacts for different people because they bring our body back into balance: under excess stress, they may raise certain hormone levels in some people. Ashwagandha, for example, has been shown to raise testosterone, and it also reduces cortisol
  - **Nettle root**, an aromatase inhibitor. Nettle is a relatively basic plant with powerful medicinal properties, but nettle leaf and nettle root are different: the leaf helps with allergies and things like that; the root basically helps prevent the conversion of as much testosterone into estrogen. A lot of us want to kind of minimize that conversion, especially when supplementing anything that may boost testosterone. A lot of people find they really feel good on it
  - **Fenugreek:** a seed and spice, used in curry, with amazing effects as a supplement. It has been shown and proven to increase testosterone, but the favorite impact is that it contains and boosts oxytocin, important for feeling good, relationships, social fears and confidence, with powerful physical effects. Basically a food, really safe
  - **DHEA:**
    - Tested for banned substances, be extra careful with any supplement and check whether DHEA is on the list: it's very safe, but probably on the NCAA's banned list
    - It's basically a grandmother or grandfather type of hormone: it acts directly in a lot of powerful ways, and it's an upstream hormone the body converts into lots of downstream ones, including androgens like testosterone. Most likely, the older we are, the more hormonally depleted, regardless of training, and the more this kind of stuff applies to us; with any endurance training, all this stuff really applies too
    - It's basically a building block, really closely linked with aging: levels decline as we age, and it's associated with sort of a youthful vitality. Personally, amazing results from it, and a lot of people find them. It seems worth trying, and absolutely recommended to check out over 35 or 40. At 22 years old, you don't need it, don't take it. It's definitely a very beneficial way to support overall hormone production, because it's literally the building blocks
    - DHEA and cortisol also have that inverse relationship: higher DHEA, lower cortisol tends to be
    - Taking DHEA, recommended: take nettle root or maca, which also has that aromatase inhibitor action, with it, so the extra building blocks stay more on the testosterone side rather than going down the estrogen side. Broccoli, cabbage and cruciferous vegetables also help prevent that conversion
- A suggestion: whatever one, two or three of these popped out in your unique circumstances, give them a try and see how they impact your life
